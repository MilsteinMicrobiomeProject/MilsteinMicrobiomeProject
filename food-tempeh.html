Tempeh

Limited evidence

Tempeh is made by fermenting soybeans with a mold called Rhizopus oligosporus, forming a firm, protein-rich cake. It is the only fermented food in this database where a fungus, not bacteria, does the fermenting. No clinical trial has studied what tempeh does to human gut microbiome composition. Lab studies using human fecal bacteria suggest soy tempeh can increase Bifidobacterium, but this has not been confirmed in people. Most commercial tempeh sold in Western countries is pasteurized, which kills all live microorganisms, making it a nutritious high-protein food but not a probiotic one.

~

Soy tempeh increases Bifidobacterium in gut simulator models, not confirmed in humans

Kuligowski et al. (2013) used a gut simulator with human fecal microbiota and found soy tempeh significantly increased Bifidobacterium.[4] This provides biological plausibility for in vivo effects but is not human evidence. Notably, bean tempeh increased E. coli in the same model, suggesting tempeh type matters for the direction of effects.

~

Isoflavone aglycone delivery may enhance equol production, metabotype-dependent

Rhizopus fermentation converts daidzein glycosides to more bioavailable aglycone forms. The aglycone form is more readily available for gut bacterial conversion to equol by Eggerthellaceae.[2] In the ~30–50% of adults who are equol producers, tempeh may be a more efficient equol precursor than unfermented soy. But this applies to only half the population.

Mechanisms

01

Rhizopus fungal fermentation profoundly modifies soybean composition

R. oligosporus produces dense white mycelium through soybeans over 30–37 hours at ~30°C, secreting proteases, amylases, lipases, and phytases. Protein digestibility increases from ~60% (raw soy) to ~85% (tempeh), and isoflavone glycosides are converted to aglycone forms.

02

Enhanced protein digestibility may reduce proteolytic colonic load

Fungal protease activity hydrolyzes soy protein in the small intestine. More efficient protein digestion may reduce the protein load reaching the colon for putrefactive fermentation, potentially reducing ammonia, p-cresol, and hydrogen sulfide production.

03

Chitin and beta-glucan from fungal mycelium, potential but unstudied prebiotic effect

Rhizopus mycelium contributes chitin and beta-glucan to tempeh composition. These fungal polysaccharides are theoretically fermentable by colonic bacteria but are not well-characterized as specific prebiotic substrates for human gut bacteria.

04

Vitamin K2 (MK-7) production, unique to tempeh among fermented foods

R. oligosporus produces menaquinone-7 (vitamin K2) during tempeh fermentation, a feature not shared by LAB-fermented foods. Tempeh-derived MK-7 may influence gut bacterial ecology by providing an electron carrier for anaerobic respiration.

03

Limitations & caveats

Note

No human RCT with microbiome as primary endpoint. All direct microbiome evidence is from in vitro gut simulator studies. Making claims about how tempeh affects the human gut microbiome based on this evidence would substantially overstate what is known.[1]

Note

Most Western commercial tempeh is pasteurized, eliminating live microorganisms. Pasteurized tempeh lacks live Rhizopus organisms but retains modified soy protein and isoflavone aglycones. It is therefore a high-protein, bioavailable soy food with prebiotic properties rather than a probiotic food. This distinction is rarely communicated on product labels.

Note

The dominant fermenter is a fungus, mechanistically distinct from all other fermented foods. Rhizopus is a mold, not a bacterium. Drawing analogies between tempeh and LAB-fermented foods for microbiome purposes is not mechanistically sound. Tempeh operates through entirely different pathways.
