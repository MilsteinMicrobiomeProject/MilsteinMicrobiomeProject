Aged Cheese

Limited evidence

Aged cheeses like cheddar, parmesan, and gruyere contain live bacteria at concentrations of millions to billions of colony-forming units per gram, predominantly starter culture strains. New strain-tracking research published in 2025 confirmed for the first time that bacteria from cheese can be detected in the stool of cheese consumers, directly demonstrating that cheese bacteria survive the journey to the colon. Whether their arrival translates into meaningful gut microbiome changes is less clear. The evidence suggests mostly transient transit rather than permanent colonization. Aged cheese also stands out for being effectively lactose-free after extended ripening.

01

Key effects on the microbiome

↑

Cheese-associated bacteria are detectable in stool of cheese consumers

Milani et al. (2025, Applied and Environmental Microbiology) performed the most rigorous cheese-microbiome" class="gut microbiome study to date, combining shotgun sequencing of 15 raw milk cheeses, gut environment simulation, and strain-tracking in human fecal samples. The key finding: cheese-associated bacterial strains were detectable in fecal samples of cheese consumers, directly demonstrating that cheese bacteria transit to the gut.[1] Streptococcus thermophilus showed the greatest persistence through gut conditions.

↑

Cheddar enriches Streptococcus and Lactococcus in gut and increases diversity in humanized mouse model

Lu et al. (2025, Foods) used human microbiota-associated germ-free mice fed a low-fat cheddar diet for 6 weeks and found that Streptococcus and Lactococcus were significantly enriched (p = 0.001 for both), with increased gut diversity.[2] This is a humanized mouse model, not a human clinical trial, but uses real human fecal microbiota in a controlled environment, making it more informative than standard rodent studies.

!

Parmesan delivers Bifidobacterium mongoliense capable of transient human gut colonization

A human pilot study by Milani et al. (2019) showed that Bifidobacterium mongoliense strains from Parmesan cheese can transiently colonize the human gut.[3] This is a small pilot study but represents one of the few direct human strain-colonization demonstrations for any specific cheese. The strains were derived from bovine gut and the surrounding production environment.

↑

Cheese contributed to the diversity increase in the Sonnenburg 2021 fermented food trial

The Wastyk/Sonnenburg 2021 Cell RCT (n = 72) found that a high-fermented food diet significantly increased microbiome diversity and reduced 19 inflammatory markers.[4] Aged cheese was one of the foods in the fermented food arm. Its individual contribution cannot be isolated, but notably the diversity increase was not attributable to fiber (cheese has essentially none), suggesting the live microbial payload contributed.

!

Cheese food matrix protects bacteria better than liquid dairy

The dense protein-fat matrix of aged cheese buffers stomach acid and physically encases bacterial cells. Standardized digestion model studies found log reductions of 0.88 to 1.93 in cheese versus 2.36 to 3.00 in fermented milk, meaning substantially more bacteria reach the colon alive from cheese than from a comparable serving of yogurt drink or milk.

Raw milk vs. pasteurized milk cheeses

The Milani et al. 2025 strain-tracking study specifically used raw milk cheeses, which harbor far more diverse and numerous bacteria than pasteurized-milk equivalents. Most cheese sold in US supermarkets is made from pasteurized milk. Findings from raw milk cheese research cannot be directly generalized to the pasteurized products most people eat.

02

Mechanisms

01

Live bacterial payload of aged cheese

Aged hard cheeses typically contain 10 million to 1 billion colony-forming units per gram of viable bacteria at the point of consumption, predominantly lactic acid bacteria. Found in yogurt, kimchi, and kefir." ., including Lactobacillus and Streptococcus. from starter cultures (S. thermophilus, Lc. lactis, L. helveticus, L. delbrueckii) and ripening cultures (L. paracasei, non-starter LAB). Raw milk cheeses contain additional environmental bacteria.

02

Selective bacterial persistence through the GI tract

Most cheese bacteria decline substantially in gut conditions, but S. thermophilus specifically maintains a presence. This is consistent with large-scale population studies finding S. thermophilus as a detectable dietary fingerprint in dairy consumers' fecal samples.

03

Near-zero lactose means no carbohydrate-based prebiotic effect

After 60 or more days of ripening, virtually all lactose in hard cheese has been consumed by starter culture bacteria. Aged cheddar, parmesan, and gruyere contain less than 0.1 grams of lactose per 100 grams. Unlike yogurt or kefir, aged cheese's gut effects are mediated by its live bacteria and protein and fat, not by carbohydrate fermentation.

04

Saturated fat and secondary bile acid consideration

Aged hard cheeses are high in saturated fat (25 to 35 grams per 100 grams). High saturated animal fat intake can enrich secondary bile acid-producing bacteria via increased bile acid secretion. Whether cheese fat triggers the same ecology shift as saturated fat in meat is not well-characterized, but multiple large prospective studies have found neutral to beneficial associations between aged cheese consumption and metabolic health.

05

Bioactive compounds from the aging process

Cheese ripening produces conjugated linoleic acid (CLA), short-chain and medium-chain fatty acids including butyrate, propionate, and octanoate, bioactive peptides, and exopolysaccharides. CLA has documented anti-inflammatory effects that may modulate gut immune signaling independently of the microbial payload.

03

Limitations & caveats

Note

No human RCT with gut microbiome as primary endpoint. Despite being one of the most widely consumed fermented foods globally, aged cheese has no dedicated human clinical trial examining gut microbiome composition. All microbiome data comes from in vitro systems, animal models, or studies where cheese was part of a broader dietary pattern.[1]

Note

Enormous variety across cheese types. Data from a raw milk Parmesan study cannot be generalized to pasteurized processed cheese slices. Aging duration, milk type, pasteurization status, starter cultures, and sodium content all differ dramatically between cheese varieties. The Milani 2025 strain-tracking data used raw milk cheeses specifically.

Note

High saturated fat and sodium. Aged cheeses are among the highest-sodium and highest-saturated-fat foods in the Western diet. These properties have their own gut ecology effects that may partially offset beneficial probiotic effects. The net gut microbiome effect of habitual aged cheese consumption, accounting for all components, is genuinely uncertain.
