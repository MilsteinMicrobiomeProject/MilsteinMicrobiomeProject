1. The evidence tier
The colored badge at the top of every food page is the most important thing on it. It tells you how much high-quality human evidence exists for that food's effects on the gut microbiome.
Strong

Multiple human trials with consistent results

The effect has been confirmed in well-designed clinical trials in people, more than once, with consistent findings.

Emerging

Promising signals, but incomplete evidence

Human studies suggest real effects, but the trials are small, short, or inconsistent enough that confidence is not yet warranted.

Limited

Little or no direct human evidence

The biology may be plausible, but controlled human trials are largely absent. The page describes what is known, honestly.

Important: Limited does not mean unhealthy. It means the food has not been rigorously studied in humans with gut microbiome as the primary outcome. Many nutritious foods have a Limited tier simply because researchers have not focused on them yet.
2. Key effects — what the icons mean
Each row in the effects section describes one documented effect on the gut microbiome. The colored circle on the left tells you the direction and confidence of that effect at a glance.
↑

Green arrow up

This food increases something beneficial: a helpful bacterial species, microbial diversity, or a protective compound like butyrate. Backed by human evidence.

↓

Green arrow down

This food decreases something harmful: a bacteria linked to inflammation or disease. A decrease in something harmful is a good thing, hence green.

!

Green exclamation

A notable positive finding that does not fit neatly as a simple increase or decrease. Often used for findings that correct a common misconception.

!

Yellow exclamation

A real finding that comes with an important condition or nuance. For example, an effect that only occurs in certain people, or a result that contradicts a simpler story.

↑

Yellow arrow

A preliminary or inconsistent finding. The direction is plausible but the evidence is not strong enough for high confidence. Often based on a single small trial or animal studies.

↑

Red arrow

This food increases something potentially harmful: a pro-inflammatory bacteria, a compound associated with disease risk, or a reduction in protective species.

~

Grey tilde

The evidence is mixed, null, or the effect simply has not been studied in humans yet. This is an honest acknowledgment of uncertainty, not a finding of "no effect."

3. Mechanisms — the how
The mechanisms section explains the step-by-step biological process: how a food survives digestion, which bacteria respond to it, and what those bacteria produce. It is written to be understandable without a science background. When a mechanism has been confirmed in humans it is stated directly. When it is inferred from animal or lab studies, that is noted. 4. Limitations & caveats
This section lists the most significant weaknesses in the evidence for each food. It is one of the most important parts of the page. A food with substantial caveats should be interpreted more cautiously than one with minor ones. Common caveats include:
Common types of caveats

Supplement vs. whole food: Many trials use concentrated extracts or purified compounds at doses far higher than you would get from eating the food normally. Results may not translate to dietary portions.

Animal studies: Effects seen in mice often do not replicate in people. When animal evidence is the main support for a claim, this is flagged.

Small sample sizes: A trial with 15 people is much weaker evidence than one with 500. Small trials are more likely to produce false positives or results that don't hold up when repeated.

Individual variation: The gut microbiome differs significantly between people. Some effects only occur in certain population groups, and this limits how broadly any finding can be applied.

Industry funding: Research funded by food companies has a higher risk of bias toward positive results. When this applies, it is flagged.

5. Evidence quality scores
The sidebar of each food page shows five scores that explain why the overall evidence tier is what it is. Each score runs from 0 to 100 and is color-coded green (strong), amber (moderate), or red (low).
Study qualityHow rigorous were the best available studies?
Consistency of findingsDo different studies agree with each other?
Human evidenceHow much comes from human trials vs. animals or labs?
Effect sizeHow large were the observed changes?
Mechanistic supportHow well is the underlying mechanism understood?
A food can score high on mechanistic support (the biology is well understood) but low on human evidence (it has not been tested in people). That combination is common and is exactly the kind of nuance these scores are meant to show.
5b. How the scores and tiers are determined
The evidence quality scores and overall tier are not produced by an algorithm. They reflect a judgment based on reviewing the available peer-reviewed literature for each food, using a consistent set of criteria across all pages. Here is exactly what goes into each score and how the overall tier is assigned.
How each of the five scores is assigned (0–100)

Study quality

Scores higher when: the best available studies are randomized controlled trials (RCTs) rather than observational studies; sample sizes are large (hundreds of participants rather than tens); studies lasted longer (months rather than weeks); interventions used whole foods rather than purified supplements; and trials were pre-registered, reducing the risk that researchers only reported favorable outcomes. Scores lower when the best evidence is animal studies, small pilot trials, or in vitro lab work.

Consistency of findings

Scores higher when multiple independent research groups find the same result: the same bacteria change in the same direction across different populations, methods, and study designs. Scores lower when studies point in opposite directions, or when one positive finding has never been replicated. A single impressive result that has never been confirmed by another lab scores low here regardless of how compelling it looks.

Human evidence

Scores based specifically on how much of the evidence comes from studies in people. Animal studies and lab studies are informative but do not count toward this score. A food with five well-designed human trials scores much higher than one whose entire evidence base comes from mouse studies, regardless of how convincing the animal findings are. This is the dimension where many foods score low: the human microbiome research base is surprisingly thin for many commonly eaten foods.

Effect size

Scores higher when the observed changes in the microbiome are large and meaningful: a substantial shift in the relative abundance of a key bacterial species, a significant change in microbial diversity, or a measurable increase in a beneficial compound like butyrate. Scores lower when changes are statistically significant (unlikely to be due to chance) but numerically tiny, or when results vary so much between individuals that the average effect is hard to interpret.

Mechanistic support

Scores higher when the biological explanation for why a food affects the microbiome is well-understood: the specific fiber or compound has been identified, the bacteria that process it have been named, and the downstream compounds they produce are known. Scores lower when an effect has been observed but nobody knows why it happens. High mechanistic support does not substitute for human evidence — it explains the biology, but a well-understood mechanism in a lab dish is not the same as a confirmed effect in people.

How the overall evidence tier is assigned

The overall tier (Strong, Emerging, or Limited) is assigned based on the pattern of the five scores taken together, with human evidence and consistency weighted most heavily. A food scores Strong when there are multiple well-designed human RCTs that consistently find the same result, with clear mechanisms. It scores Emerging when human trials exist and suggest real effects, but the evidence has meaningful gaps: trials are small, durations are short, results are inconsistent across studies, or effects depend heavily on supplement doses that are unrealistic from food alone. It scores Limited when human trial data are largely absent and the case for a food's microbiome effects rests mainly on animal studies, lab work, or mechanistic reasoning.

High mechanistic support alone is not enough for a higher tier. A food can have a beautifully understood biological mechanism and still be rated Limited if that mechanism has never been confirmed in a controlled human trial. This is an intentional choice: the site reflects what has been tested in people, not just what is theoretically plausible.

6. Sources
Every claim on a food page is cited. The amber numbers in the text link to the source at the bottom of the page. Each citation shows the study type, which tells you how much weight to give it. From strongest to weakest:
Study type hierarchy
Strongest Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of multiple human trials; large pre-registered randomized controlled trials
Moderate Single well-designed human clinical trials; large observational studies in humans
Supporting Small human trials; cross-sectional surveys; lab studies using human fecal bacteria
Context only Animal studies; standard cell culture; mechanistic biochemistry. These explain the biology but do not support human health claims on their own.
