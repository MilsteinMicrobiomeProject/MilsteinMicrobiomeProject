Sauerkraut

Limited evidence

Sauerkraut got its first large, rigorous clinical trial in 2025. Researchers studied 87 healthy adults and found that gut microbiomes were largely unchanged by regular sauerkraut consumption: only individual species shifted, with no change in overall diversity. One unexpected finding: pasteurized sauerkraut, which contains no live bacteria, raised blood levels of beneficial short-chain fatty acids more than fresh sauerkraut did. This suggests the prebiotic fiber and acids in sauerkraut may matter more than its live bacteria, which challenges the common assumption that unpasteurized is always better.

Schropp et al. Pasteurized sauerkraut increases serum scfa-short-chain-fatty-acid>SCFAs more than fresh, an unexpected finding

Schropp et al. (2025) found that only pasteurized sauerkraut consumption increased serum scfa-short-chain-fatty-acid>SCFAs, not fresh (live-bacteria) sauerkraut.[1] This counterintuitive result suggests sauerkraut fermentation-produced organic acids and modified fibers may reach systemic circulation more effectively than live bacteria-mediated fermentation. Pasteurization may modify the food matrix in ways that enhance prebiotic substrate fermentability.

↓

Both forms of sauerkraut reduce IBS severity, mechanism likely non-microbiome

Pinto-Sanchez et al. (2017, RCT, n = 34 IBS patients, 6 weeks) found both pasteurized and unpasteurized sauerkraut significantly reduced IBS severity scores. Crucially, this improvement did not correlate with microbiome compositional changes, suggesting organic acids directly soothing the intestinal epithelium may underlie the symptom benefit.[2]

Mechanisms

01

Lactic acid and organic acids lower colonic pH

Sauerkraut contains high concentrations of lactic acid, acetic acid, and other organic acids from LAB fermentation. These acids lower colonic pH when consumed, selectively favoring acid-tolerant beneficial bacteria over pathobiont style="text-decoration:underline;text-decoration-style:dotted;text-decoration-color:var(--amber);color:inherit;"mechanism-step">

02

Modified fiber as prebiotic substrate (may explain pasteurized > fresh finding)

Fermentation partially degrades cabbage complex polysaccharides into shorter-chain oligosaccharides that may be more readily fermentable by colonic bacteria. This mechanism, not reliant on live bacteria, may explain why pasteurized sauerkraut produced the SCFA serum increase in the Schropp et al. trial.[1]

Live LAB transit (fresh sauerkraut only, limited in healthy adults)

L. plantarum and L. mesenteroides in fresh sauerkraut survive gastric transit and interact with the resident microbiome. However, as the Schropp et al. data indicate, this transit does not reliably shift overall community composition in already-healthy adults with a robust baseline microbiome.

03

Limitations & caveats

Note

The best available evidence found “resilient” microbiome responses in healthy adults. The Schropp et al. (2025) study, the first large, rigorous sauerkraut microbiome RCT, found limited compositional changes, challenging the popular narrative that sauerkraut substantially benefits gut microbiota in healthy people.[1]

Note

Only a 4-week intervention; effects in dysbiotic populations may differ. Schropp et al. enrolled healthy adults with robust baseline microbiomes. Effects in populations with dysbiosis (IBS, IBD, metabolic syndrome) may be more pronounced. Longer interventions might also show different results.

Note

The pasteurized > fresh finding needs replication. This counterintuitive result is from a single study and requires independent replication before it should inform dietary recommendations. Multiple mechanisms could explain it, and the most important one is currently unclear.
