Coffee

Emerging evidence

Coffee has the strongest population-level association with the gut microbiome of any single food or beverage identified to date. A landmark 2024 Nature Microbiology study of 22,867 people found coffee consumption was the single highest-correlating dietary item with microbiome composition. Its signature is a bacterium called Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus, which is 4.5 to 8 times more abundant in coffee drinkers than non-drinkers. The effect persists with decaffeinated coffee, pointing to polyphenols rather than caffeine as the driver. Evidence is Emerging because most findings are observational, not from clinical trials.

The Lawsonibacter story

L. asaccharolyticus was only isolated in 2018. Whether its higher abundance in coffee drinkers is beneficial, neutral, or harmful is unknown. This is a clear example of a robust observational finding where the clinical significance is not yet established.

02

Mechanisms

01

Chlorogenic acid reaches the colon and is metabolized by gut bacteria

Only ~33% of chlorogenic acid is absorbed in the small intestine. Colonic bacteria express esterases and hydroxycinnamic acid reductases that hydrolyze it to caffeic acid, quinic acid, and phenylpropionic acid.

02

Quinic acid derivatives selectively stimulate Lawsonibacter

Quinic acid and its derivatives appear to selectively stimulate L. asaccharolyticus via unknown enzyme pathways. This bacterium has a unique metabolic niche adapted to coffee-derived substrates.

03

Coffee melanoidins act as prebiotic-like substrates

Melanoidins, high-molecular-weight polymers formed during roasting, are non-digestible and arrive in the colon as fermentable substrates supporting Bacteroides and SCFA-producing Lachnospiraceae producers. Caffeine is absorbed before reaching bacteria, not the driver

Caffeine is ~99% absorbed in the small intestine and has minimal direct contact with colonic bacteria. The decaf persistence of effects confirms caffeine plays little role.

03

Limitations & caveats

Note

The landmark finding is observational, not causal. The Nature Microbiology 2024 study establishes a strong association but cannot prove coffee causes the microbiome difference. Coffee drinkers may differ from non-drinkers in many other lifestyle ways.[1]

Note

The function of Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus is not yet characterized. Whether higher abundance is beneficial, neutral, or harmful is unknown. It was only isolated in 2018 and has not been studied for health effects in humans.

Note

Preparation method affects polyphenol content substantially. Filtered coffee vs. French press vs. espresso differ in diterpene content. Roast level, brewing time, and water temperature all affect chlorogenic acid and melanoidin content.
