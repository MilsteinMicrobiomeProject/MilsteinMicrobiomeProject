Miso

Limited evidence

Miso is a fermented soybean paste made using a mold called Aspergillus oryzae. No published clinical trial has measured what miso does to gut microbiome composition. What is known is that miso transforms soy compounds called isoflavones into forms that some gut bacteria can convert to equol, a compound with potential anti-inflammatory effects. However, only about 30 to 50 percent of adults in Western countries carry the specific bacteria needed to complete this conversion. Miso is also high in sodium, containing 9 to 12 percent salt, worth considering alongside any potential gut benefits.

Key effects on the microbiome

Equol production from daidzein, but only in ~30–50% of adults

Miso contains daidzein isoflavones, which gut bacteria convert to equol in people who harbor equol-producing bacteria (Adlercreutzia equolifaciens, Slackia isoflavoniconvertens). Aspergillus oryzae beta-glucosidases convert daidzein glycosides to the more bioavailable aglycone form during fermentation, making miso a more effective equol precursor than unfermented tofu.[3] But only ~30–50% of Western adults carry equol-producing bacteria, this benefit applies to approximately half the population.

~

Fermentation modifies oligosaccharide prebiotic profile

Aspergillus oryzae enzymatic activity hydrolyzes anti-nutritional oligosaccharides (raffinose, stachyose) in soybeans to simpler sugars and short-chain oligosaccharides. This potentially modifies the prebiotic substrate profile reaching the colon. No direct human microbiome study has characterized this effect for miso specifically.

Mechanisms

01

Aspergillus enzymatic transformation enhances bioavailability

A. oryzae produces a broad spectrum of enzymes (proteases, amylases, lipases, phytases, beta-glucosidases) that transform soybean components, increasing protein digestibility, reducing anti-nutrients, and converting isoflavone glycosides to more bioavailable aglycones.

02

Equol production pathway, microbiome-dependent

Daidzein → equol via Eggerthellaceae, mediated by daidzein reductase, dihydrodaidzein reductase, and tetrahydrodaidzein reductase enzyme cascade. Only individuals harboring these specific bacteria complete the full conversion, making miso health effects fundamentally dependent on the consumer microbiome composition.

03

High salt content, a potential counteracting mechanism

Miso is 9–12% sodium chloride, which independently modulates gut bacterial communities, high salt reduces Lactobacillus in animal models with immune consequences. Typical miso consumption (1–2 tbsp/day) delivers 600–1200 mg sodium. The high salt content could partially counteract potential fermentation benefits.

03

Limitations & caveats

Note

No human RCT has measured miso effects on microbiomecaveat-item">Note

Equol benefits apply only to ~30–50% of adults in Western populations. Higher equol production frequency in Japan (~50–60%) limits generalization of Japanese miso health data to Western populations. The isoflavone/equol benefits are inherently microbiome-dependent and population-specific.

Note

High sodium content is a real clinical concern. Regular miso consumption at typical quantities delivers 600–1200 mg sodium/day, a significant consideration for individuals managing blood pressure. Potential microbiome benefits (unproven) must be weighed against this established dietary risk.
